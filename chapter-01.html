📊 Chapter 1: Principles of Data Science and the Analytics Lifecycle

Every scroll, swipe, tap, and GCash payment you make today becomes data. This chapter is your starter pack: what data science actually is, how raw data becomes real decisions, and the lifecycle that every legit data project follows.

🎓 Lecture • Dr. Ryan Manuel D. Guido
Sources: Herman, Chs. 1–2 · Plaue, Intro. & Ch. 1 · Grus, Ch. 1

1.1 Why Data Science? (Real Talk) 💬

Think about your morning so far. Your phone's algorithm chose which videos you saw. Your ride app estimated your fare and ETA. The store where you bought breakfast logged the sale in its inventory system. None of that runs on vibes — it runs on data being collected, cleaned, analyzed, and turned into decisions.

Data science is the interdisciplinary field that extracts insights and knowledge from data using scientific and computational methods — a mash-up of statistics, computer science, and domain expertise (Herman, Ch. 1; Grus, Ch. 1). Grus describes a data scientist as someone who finds insights hidden in messy, real-world data. Organizations use it for data-driven decision-making, which Herman (Ch. 1) says helps them:

📈 Spot market trendsand consumer preferences before competitors do
⚙️ Boost operational efficiencyfind bottlenecks and fix workflows
🛡️ Mitigate risksquantify uncertainty instead of guessing
💡 Drive innovationnew products and competitive advantage

Source: Herman, Ch. 1 (Introduction to Data-Driven Decision-Making); Grus, Ch. 1.

🤔 Think about this: A milk tea chain notices matcha sales spiking every time a certain K-drama trends. Is that "data science" yet? Not quite — noticing is step zero. Data science is the systematic process that confirms the pattern, measures it, and turns it into a supply decision.

1.2 Key Concepts and Terminology 🔑

Herman (Ch. 1) defines the core vocabulary you'll use all semester:

Dataraw facts and figures — numbers, text, images, clicks — not yet meaningful on their own
Big Datadatasets so large and complex that traditional tools can't handle them
Data Typesstructured (tables), unstructured (text, images, video), and everything in between
Machine Learningalgorithms that learn patterns from data instead of following hand-written rules
Predictive Analyticsusing historical data to forecast what happens next
Data Visualizationturning numbers into charts people can actually understand
Algorithma step-by-step procedure a computer follows to solve a problem

Descriptive vs. Inferential Statistics

🧾 Descriptive

Summarizes the data you have.

  • "Average screen time in our survey: 7.2 hours."
  • "Store B sold the most last month."

🔮 Inferential

Generalizes from a sample to a bigger population.

  • "Based on 500 respondents, we estimate 62% of ALL students prefer online enrollment."

Source: Herman, Chs. 1–2 (Key Concepts; Statistical Fundamentals).

1.3 The Data Science Lifecycle 🔄

Herman (Ch. 1) presents the data science lifecycle — the roadmap every serious data project follows. Skipping a stage is how projects flop.

1
📥
Data Acquisitioncollect data from sources: databases, surveys, sensors, APIs, web
2
🧹
Data Preparationclean, transform, and organize raw data into analysis-ready form
3
🔍
Exploratory Data Analysissummarize, visualize, and understand patterns before modeling
4
🤖

5
✅
Model Evaluationtest performance on unseen data — does it actually work?
6
🚀
Deployment & Monitoringput the model into use and watch its performance over time
↺ Monitoring feeds back into new data and better models — it's a loop, not a line.

Why the loop matters: a model that predicted well in June can drift by December — user behavior changes, new products launch, a pandemic happens. That's why deployment and monitoring is a stage, not an afterthought (Herman, Ch. 1).

Source: Herman, Ch. 1 (The Data Science Lifecycle).

📱 Case snapshot (Herman, Ch. 1): A retail chain used the lifecycle to optimize its supply chain — acquiring sales and inventory data per store, preparing and exploring it, then modeling demand against market trends. The visual analyses of sales quantity vs. inventory levels by store guided restocking decisions. Same playbook, whether it's a mega-retailer or your org's merch booth.

1.4 The Three Pillars of Data Science 🏛️

Plaue (Introduction) organizes the whole field into three pillars — and conveniently, they map to the three parts of our course:

🗂️ Data Organization

Structuring data conceptually and logically, ensuring its quality, and summarizing it with descriptive statistics.

🎲 Stochastics

Probability theory + inferential statistics — the foundation for reasoning under uncertainty.

🧠 Machine Learning

Algorithms that learn rules from training data instead of being explicitly programmed.

Source: Plaue, Introduction.

1.5 Data Collection: Sources, Techniques, and Quality 📥

Herman (Ch. 2) calls data collection "the backbone of data science." Three things to master:

Data sources. Internal databases, public datasets, surveys, sensors and IoT devices, web platforms and social media.

Acquisition techniques. Querying databases, running surveys, web scraping, connecting to APIs, logging from sensors.

Quality and integrity. Garbage in, garbage out. Plaue (Secs. 1.3) breaks quality into dimensions — is the data accurate? complete? consistent? current? If your dataset says a student weighs 650 kg, no fancy model can save the analysis.

Source: Herman, Ch. 2 (Data Collection); Plaue, Secs. 1.1–1.3 (Data organization and quality).

1.6 Data Cleaning: The Unskippable Grind 🧹

Real-world data is messy — typos, blanks, duplicates, "N/A" written five different ways. Plaue (Sec. 1.4) gives the five core cleaning operations:

1️⃣ Validationdetect impossible or implausible values (age = 190, negative sales)
2️⃣ Standardizationput values in consistent formats (dates, units, spellings, categories)
3️⃣ Imputationfill in missing values with reasonable estimates instead of throwing rows away
4️⃣ Augmentationenrich or expand the data — e.g., generating additional training examples
5️⃣ Deduplicationfind and merge duplicate records so nothing is counted twice

Source: Plaue, Sec. 1.4; Herman, Ch. 2 (Data Preprocessing).

1.7 Inference vs. Prediction 🔮

Herman (Ch. 2) distinguishes the two big goals of analysis — you'll meet this distinction again and again:

🧪 Inference

Understanding relationships in a population: "Did the feeding program actually improve attendance, or is the difference just chance?" Inference generalizes from samples while accounting for uncertainty.

🎯 Prediction

Forecasting individual future outcomes: "Which of tomorrow's orders will be cancelled?" We may care less about why and more about being right.

Both rely on the same statistical foundations — probability distributions, hypothesis testing, regression, and statistical inference (Herman, Ch. 2) — which are exactly what Chapters 2–4 will review.

Source: Herman, Ch. 2 (Inference and Prediction; Statistical Fundamentals).

1.8 Key Takeaways 🎒

✅ Data science = statistics + computing + domain knowledge, aimed at insight and better decisions.
✅ The lifecycle has six stages: acquisition → preparation → EDA → model development → evaluation → deployment & monitoring — and it loops.
✅ The field stands on three pillars: data organization, stochastics, machine learning.
✅ Data quality is everything; cleaning = validation, standardization, imputation, augmentation, deduplication.
✅ Descriptive summarizes what you have; inferential generalizes beyond it. Inference explains; prediction forecasts.
