          #A. Chapter Opener

          #An Ordinary Morning, Quietly Recorded

          Think about the first two hours of your day. Before you even reached campus, a surprising number of machines were writing things down about you. Websites track every user’s every click. A smartphone builds up a record of its owner’s location and speed every second of every day. Smart cars collect driving habits, smart homes collect living habits, and marketers collect purchasing habits.

          None of this is unusual. What is unusual is how much of it goes unexamined. Buried in these records are answers to countless questions that no one has ever thought to ask. Somebody has to ask them, and somebody has to know how to get an honest answer out of a messy pile of records. That somebody is a data analyst, and this chapter is where you begin learning to be one.

          Consider a concrete case. A retail chain wanted to stop running out of popular items while also not overstocking slow-moving ones. It began by collecting data from three places at once: its sales transactions, its inventory levels, and information about market trends. It then cleaned and transformed that raw data. Only afterwards did it look for patterns in demand, and only after that did it change how it restocked its branches. The result was less excess inventory, fewer stockouts, and a more efficient supply chain.

          Notice what the retail chain did not do. It did not look at one busy weekend and guess. It followed a sequence, and the sequence is the whole point of this chapter.

          Essential Question

          How does raw, messy data become knowledge that a person can actually act on — and what has to be true about the data, the process, and the analyst for that knowledge to be trustworthy?

          Big Idea

          Data by itself decides nothing. Data becomes useful only when it is deliberately collected, understood as a particular type, organized, checked for quality, cleaned, analysed, interpreted, and communicated — in that order, and with care at every step. This ordered journey is called the data life cycle, and the discipline that carries data through it is data analytics.

          #Why This Lesson Matters

          The era of relying solely on intuition or limited information is over. Organizations of every size now use data to identify market trends and consumer preferences, enhance operational efficiency, mitigate risks and uncertainties, and drive innovation and competitive advantage. That shift is not confined to one industry. Retailers use analytics to personalise recommendations, healthcare providers use it to support diagnosis and treatment planning, financial services use it to detect fraudulent transactions, and manufacturers use it to predict equipment failure before it happens.

          This matters to you for a simple reason: you are already surrounded by claims that say "the data shows." Some of those claims are careful. Some are careless. Some are dishonest. Without the vocabulary and the habits in this chapter, you cannot tell the difference — and a person who cannot tell the difference is easy to mislead.

          #Lesson 1.1 What Is Data Analytics?

          Start with the smaller word. According to the International Organization for Standardization, data are "a reinterpretable representation of information in a formalized manner, suitable for communication, interpretation, or processing." A second, plainer description says that data are "factual information (such as measurements or statistics) used as a basis for reasoning, discussion, or calculation". A third source puts it more bluntly still: data are raw facts, figures, and statistics collected for analysis.

          Read those definitions again and notice what they have in common. Not one of them says that data are true, or important, or meaningful. Data are a raw material. They are the clay, not the brick.

          Now the larger word. Data analysis aims to obtain relevant information and useful knowledge by systematic organization, statistical processing, and/or graphical representation of data — which in this context is also called raw data. The wider discipline is described this way: the collection, processing, interpretation, and communication of data with the goal of obtaining robust and useful knowledge, usually with the aid of information technology, is the main function of data science.

          That sentence is worth memorising, because it contains the whole subject in miniature. There are four verbs — collect, process, interpret, communicate — and one goal: robust and useful knowledge. Everything in the rest of this chapter is one of those four verbs done carefully.

          A working definition for this chapter

          Definition

          Data analytics is the systematic practice of collecting, processing, interpreting, and communicating data in order to obtain relevant information and useful knowledge that supports informed and evidence-based decisions.

          The person who does this work has been described very simply: a data scientist is someone who extracts insights from messy data. Hold on to the word "messy." It is not a complaint; it is a job description. Real data arrives incomplete, inconsistent, misspelled, and duplicated, and the analyst’s competence is measured largely by what they do about that.

          Data, information, and knowledge

          The three definitions above draw a ladder that is easy to miss. Data are the formalized representation. Information is what the data mean once they are organized and interpreted. Knowledge is what you can now justifiably act on. Data analysis is the climb from the first rung to the third.

          An example makes the ladder visible. The figure 47 is data. "Section A had 47 absences in September, which is the highest of any section" is information. "Section A’s absences concentrate on Mondays after payday weekends, so a Monday intervention is worth trying" is knowledge — and note that the last statement required organizing, summarizing, and interpreting, not merely reading a number.

          #Lesson 1.2 Why Data Matters

          Data has emerged as a cornerstone of strategic decision-making, and the era of relying solely on intuition or limited information is over. That is a strong claim, so it deserves specifics. Four benefits are given for the organizations that make decisions from data:

          BenefitWhat it means in practice
          Identify market trends and consumer preferencesBy analysing large datasets, an organization uncovers insights into trends, behaviour, and emerging opportunities, so it can tailor what it offers and respond to changing demand instead of being surprised by it.
          Enhance operational efficiencyAnalytics optimizes processes, improves resource allocation, and streamlines operations, which reduces cost and waste and raises productivity.
          Mitigate risks and uncertaintiesPredictive analytics and risk modelling let an organization anticipate challenges and identify vulnerabilities early, so it can act before the loss occurs rather than after.
          Drive innovation and competitive advantageInsights that others do not have allow an organization to develop new offerings and find niches, establishing an edge over competitors.

          The same skill, five different industries

          The same underlying practice appears across sectors:

          • Retail. A recommendation engine analyses a customer’s past purchases, browsing history, and saved items in order to predict which products they are likely to want.
          • Healthcare. Analytics applied to clinical data, medical literature, and patient records supports diagnosis and treatment planning, and helps identify effective treatment options.
          • Finance. A fraud detection system analyses transaction history, user behaviour patterns, and real-time payment activity to identify anomalies such as unusual amounts or atypical access locations, and blocks suspicious transactions before they complete.
          • Manufacturing. Predictive maintenance analyses sensor data — temperature, vibration, pressure — to predict when equipment is likely to fail, so maintenance is scheduled before the breakdown.
          • Entertainment. Analytics informs content recommendations and personalised marketing.

          Read that list once more and look for the pattern rather than the industries. In each case, historical records are used to answer a forward-looking question, and the answer changes an action. That is the shape of the work everywhere it appears.

          Classroom Scenario (a constructed illustration)

          A university canteen throws away a large amount of unsold food every Friday. The canteen manager has eight weeks of sales records showing, for each day, the number of servings prepared and the number sold per menu item. Used well, those records serve three of the four benefits above at once: they reveal a demand pattern (trends), they reduce waste (operational efficiency), and they reduce the risk of a large loss on any single day (risk mitigation).

          Note carefully: this scenario is a classroom illustration built to apply the concepts of this lesson. It is not a reported case study, and no figures are claimed for it.

          #Lesson 1.3 Types of Data

          This lesson develops the second course outcome: differentiate data types. It matters more than it first appears. The data type for a variable determines what visual display, what analysis, and what statistical model are appropriate, and it even determines how software will handle computations for that variable. Choosing the wrong type is not a labelling error; it is the beginning of a wrong answer.

          First division: structured or unstructured

          Much of the world’s data is unstructured. Images are collections of pixels, each pixel carrying colour information. Texts are sequences of words and non-word characters, organized into sections. Clickstreams are sequences of actions taken by a user interacting with an app or a web page. A major challenge of data science is to harness this torrent of raw data into actionable information, and to do that, unstructured raw data must be processed and manipulated into a structured form.

          The commonest structured form is a table with rows and columns, as data might emerge from a database or be collected for a study. Almost everything you will do this term begins by getting your data into that shape.

          Second division: numeric or categorical

          There are two basic types of structured data: numeric and categorical.

          TypeDefinitionSub-typesExample
          Numeric (quantitative)Data expressed on a numeric scale. A variable is numeric when comparisons of its values as differences or ratios are meaningful — "this person is 10 cm taller than that person" makes sense.Continuous: can take any value in an interval (wind speed, time duration). Discrete: takes only integer values, such as counts.Body height in centimetres; sales quantity; temperature in degrees Celsius
          Categorical (qualitative)Data that can take on only a specific set of values representing categories. A variable that is either nominal or ordinal is called categorical or qualitative.Nominal: finitely many values with no natural order. Ordinal: values with a meaningful order. Binary: exactly two values.Store Location; Market Trends recorded as stable, increasing, or decreasing; Failure recorded as 0 or 1

          Nominal, ordinal, and binary, precisely

          Variables that may take only a finite number of values which have no natural, meaningful order are called nominal variables; a person’s sex is a nominal variable. Variables that produce a finite number of possible outcomes which can be ordered in some natural and meaningful way are called ordinal variables; an income category numbered 1 to 8 is ordinal, because a person placed in category 6 earns more than a person in category 3. Binary data is an important special case of categorical data that takes on only one of two values, such as 0 and 1, yes and no, or true and false.

          A useful test separates nominal from ordinal in one question: if you shuffled the category labels into a different order, would you lose information? Shuffling "male, female" loses nothing. Shuffling income categories 1 through 8 destroys the meaning of the variable. That is the difference.

          Other useful distinctions

          • Temporal data. Data can also be categorized as temporal, for example timestamps. The Timestamp column in an equipment monitoring dataset is of this kind.
          • Binary variables and subpopulations. Each subpopulation uniquely defines a binary variable: a unit is assigned 1 if it belongs to the subpopulation and 0 if it does not, and conversely each binary variable defines a subpopulation. This is why so many analyses reduce to a single 0/1 column.
          • Univariate, bivariate, multivariate. A univariate variable gives one value per observation. Several univariate variables can be combined into a multivariate variable to yield feature vectors; one with exactly two entries is bivariate, such as (height in m, body weight in kg).

          Why this classification earns its keep

          The taxonomy exists for a practical reason: for the purposes of data analysis and predictive modelling, the data type helps determine the type of visual display, data analysis, or statistical model to use, and data science software uses these types to improve computational performance.

          Concretely: you may compute a mean of a numeric variable, but the mean of nominal codes is meaningless. You may rank ordinal categories, but you may not assume the gap between category 3 and 4 equals the gap between 7 and 8. Getting the type right is what stops you from computing a number that looks respectable and means nothing.

          #Lesson 1.4 Where Data Comes From and How It Is Organized

          Data collection is the initial step in the data science process, laying the foundation for all subsequent analysis and decision-making. Everything downstream inherits the strengths and the defects of this step, which is why it is treated first and treated seriously.

          Sources of data

          Data can be sourced from a variety of places:

          SourceDescription
          Internal databasesRecords the organization already holds: customer records, sales transactions, operational logs.
          External APIsInterfaces provided by outside services that offer access to large amounts of real-time data.
          Web scrapingExtracting information from websites.
          Sensor networksGathering data from physical environments — for example, sensors installed on machinery recording temperature, vibration, and pressure.
          Social media platformsSources of insight into user behaviour and trends.
          Third-party data providersOrganizations that supply curated datasets.

          One principle deserves to be underlined: understanding the availability, reliability, and legality of these data sources is crucial for ensuring the quality and relevance of the collected data. Legality is listed alongside reliability, not after it. A dataset you were not entitled to obtain is not a shortcut; it is a defect you cannot fix later.

          Acquisition techniques

          Different techniques may be employed depending on the nature of the data and the desired outcomes: manual data entry, in which data is input by individuals; automated data collection scripts, which gather data without human intervention; data streaming, which captures and processes data in real time; and real-time data ingestion pipelines, which integrate data from various sources as it arrives. Each technique has advantages and challenges — the accuracy and timeliness of manual entry weighed against the efficiency and scalability of automated methods.

          How an organized dataset is built

          Once data is gathered it must be structured. The vocabulary is precise:

          1. A data item is the smallest organizational unit of data — one value in one cell.
          2. A dataset is a collection of data items that are structured in the same way.
          3. Each row of a data table is a data record, also called a data tuple; it represents one entity.
          4. Each column corresponds to an attribute, and the attribute values in a single row constitute one record.
          5. The primary key attribute uniquely identifies each entity described by a record. It may be as simple as a consecutive sequence of positive integers.

          The same object viewed statistically has parallel names: the rows represent statistical units, entities, or objects, and the number of statistical units is called the sample size; a column represents a series of measurements of a single variable. In data science practice, this two-dimensional arrangement is called rectangular data, with rows indicating records and columns indicating features.

          It is tempting to think the work ends when an answer is produced.           #Lesson 1.6 Judging Data Quality

          The principal purpose of data science is to generate trusted and useful knowledge from data, and a high-quality dataset is a prerequisite for fulfilling that purpose. This lesson gives you a way to judge quality that is more disciplined than "it looks fine."

          The six data quality dimensions

          Quality is assessed along six key characteristics called data quality dimensions: completeness, consistency, validity, uniqueness, accuracy, and timeliness. Each is best remembered as a question you ask of a dataset.

          DimensionThe question it asksA typical defect
          CompletenessHave all relevant data been collected and stored?Some persons in the customer database are missing address information.
          ConsistencyAre redundantly stored data consistent, or does the information contradict itself?Customer addresses stored at a branch differ from those stored at the central office.
          ValidityAre the data formally correct — syntactically correct, of the correct type, within the valid range of values?A postal code contains invalid characters, such as letters in "123Q5".
          UniquenessCan the data associated with any given entity be uniquely identified?Several different primary keys have been assigned to the same person.
          AccuracyDo the data reflect the truth, or are they at odds with reality?For some customers, the name or address is wrong.
          TimelinessAre the data up to date for the use they are being put to?Records that have not been refreshed since the situation they describe changed.

          A parallel account in a second source lists accuracy, completeness, consistency, and timeliness as the properties to validate, defining accuracy as ensuring the data reflects real-world scenarios correctly, completeness as ensuring no critical data is missing, consistency as ensuring data is uniform across the dataset, and timeliness as ensuring data is up to date. The two accounts agree; the first simply adds validity and uniqueness as separate dimensions.

          Quality is relative to the use, not absolute

          A principle worth arguing about

          Data quality measures the extent to which a dataset can be used as intended. Consequently, the significance of each data quality dimension and the priority with which data defects need to be managed depend on the use case at hand.

          A sharp illustration: validity matters greatly for automated processing, because invalid inputs can cause software errors if they are not caught. Accuracy, by contrast, plays only a minor role at the automated processing stage — but it is a vital feature for information presented to and evaluated by a human.

          The lesson is uncomfortable and important: there is no such thing as a clean dataset in the abstract. There is only a dataset clean enough for a stated purpose. Ask what the data is for before you decide what counts as a defect.

          Data profiling and documentation

          Data profiling is the practice of collecting metrics in order to assess data quality. The metrics are usually simple counts or proportions — for example, the number, proportion, or percentage of customers with missing address information, or the proportion of records with an invalid postal code, calculated by matching against a correct list or against a fixed pattern such as exactly five digits with no letters. You can do this with nothing more than a pencil and a tally.

          Completeness of metadata is also a relevant indicator of high data quality, especially for documentation, because good data documentation ensures reproducibility of results and transparency of the analysis process. Documentation may include the context of collection, the underlying hypotheses and goals; the meaning of the data and its structure; a description of attributes including their range of valid values and units of measurement; an evaluation of data quality and a description of any cleaning performed; the location, time, and method of collection; information about versioning; technical information; and terms of use and data protection measures.

          #Lesson 1.7 Preparing and Cleaning Data

          Raw data collected from various sources often requires preprocessing to make it suitable for analysis. This lesson is the practical heart of the chapter, and it is where the performance standard of the subject is met: students process data accurately and securely by creating backups, cleaning datasets to address missing or inconsistent entries, and converting data formats based on specified requirements.

          Rule zero: protect the original

          Before changing anything, preserve what you were given. It is highly recommended to keep a historical record of preprocessing tasks, which may include maintaining the original raw data with the database, or a backup of it. Such a historical record is called data provenance, and it helps with tracking down the cause of data quality issues.

          This is why standard practice requires creating backups of data to maintain security and track changes. Cleaning is destructive: every correction you make destroys evidence of what the data originally said. Provenance is what allows you to undo a mistake and, just as importantly, what allows someone else to check you.

          The five cleaning operations

          Data cleaning comprises five core operations. Learn them as a repertoire; a real dataset usually needs several.

          1. Validation

          Invalid or implausible attribute values can be identified by checking against validation rules and plausibility rules; removing invalid values or converting them into a valid format may be part of the process. Rules come in three flavours:

          • Syntax rules. A birth date may be required in the format YYYY-MM-DD, where YYYY is four digits, MM is two digits with a leading zero if needed, and the day is two digits. Under this rule 2010-12-01 is valid, but 10-12-01 and 2010-12-1 are not.
          • Range rules. The value 2010-23-01 satisfies the syntax rule but fails once the range condition 1 ≤ MM ≤ 12 is added.
          • Plausibility rules. A birth date can be marked invalid when it lies in the future at the time the dataset was created, or lies impossibly far in the past.

          In many cases invalid or implausible values are handled by deleting them; in other cases a transformation corrects the defect, for example inserting a leading zero to turn YYYY-MM-D into YYYY-MM-DD. A caution worth taking seriously: such a repair is only advisable if the data’s provenance and the way it was generated imply that the result is not only valid but also accurate. A value that now passes your checks is not thereby true.

          2. Standardization

          Data standardization aims to align a heterogeneous syntax or units of measurement; attribute values are converted into a specified target syntax and common units of measurement. In practice this is what it means to convert data formats and types consistently and accurately based on specific requirements.

          Table C.3. Heterogeneous, non-standardized data: two records describing the same person

          3. Imputation

          Missing attribute values occur for many reasons: a respondent may have refused to provide information, a measuring device may have malfunctioned, or values may have been discarded during validation. Data imputation is the process of completing data by replacing missing attribute values with synthetic but plausible values.

          Two principles govern how you should think about it. First, missing attribute values can lead to a bias in the results of data analysis. Second, the primary goal of imputation is not to determine the correct attribute values but to supplement the data in order to improve the results. Imputation is damage control, not truth recovery. Say so when you report it.

          The main alternative is complete case analysis, a simple and commonly used procedure in which records missing relevant values are excluded from the analysis. A second source lists the substitution methods in common use: filling gaps using mean, median, or mode substitution, or more advanced techniques such as multiple imputation or using algorithms to predict the missing values.

          Worked Example 2 in Part D carries out mean substitution on real numbers, including the refinement of using imputation classes.

          4. Augmentation

          Sometimes the problem is not a wrong value but too few values. Data coverage can be increased by tapping into new data sources and collecting more data; in machine learning, synthetic data is also used to provide additional training data. Data augmentation refers to an extension of the dataset by artificially generated data records, typically generated by a suitable transformation of already existing records.

          As with imputation, augmentation is not about adding accurate data; it aims to avoid bias in the results caused by lack of data, and it can improve the robustness of a model trained on the data. In image or video analysis, available images can be subjected to geometric transformations such as shear, zoom, reflection, or rotation, or have their sharpness, contrast, brightness, or colour temperature changed to create additional images. In text analysis, techniques include replacing words with synonyms or using back translation.

          5. Deduplication

          Data deduplication is the task of identifying the data records that describe a single entity in order to merge them into a single representative data record. The related terms object identification, entity resolution, and record linkage describe the same task.

          A memorable real illustration comes from a patent database, in which one company appears as SIEMENES AKTIENGESELLSCHAFT; Siemens; Siemens A.G.; Siemens AG; SIEMENS AG (DE); SIEMENS AG, 8000 MUNICH, DE; Siemens Akteingesellschaft; Siemens Aktiengellschaft; and several further misspellings. The causes of this heterogeneity are typographic errors, abbreviated spellings, address information included with the name, and transcription. As a consequence, even determining the number of patents registered by that company becomes a non-trivial task, since the organization is not represented in a unique way.

          Read that consequence carefully, because it generalises. Duplication does not merely make a dataset untidy. It makes counting wrong — and almost every analysis begins with a count.

          Transformation and integration

          Two further preparation activities complete the picture. Data transformation converts raw data into a more suitable format: standardizing units of measurement so all data points are comparable, encoding categorical variables into numerical values, scaling numerical features so they fall in a similar range, and reducing the number of variables while retaining essential information. Data integration combines data from multiple sources into a unified dataset by resolving inconsistencies in formats such as date formats, aligning data schemas so fields from different sources correspond correctly, and merging datasets on common identifiers.

          The order that keeps you out of trouble

          There is no single prescribed sequence, but the operations have natural dependencies. Back up the raw data first. Validate next, because validation may create new missing values by removing invalid ones. Standardize before deduplicating, because records that look different only because of format will not be recognised as duplicates until the format is unified. Deduplicate before summarizing, because duplicates corrupt counts. Impute last among the corrections, since earlier steps change which values are missing. Augment only if coverage is genuinely insufficient.

          #Lesson 1.8 Types of Data Analysis

          This lesson completes the course outcome on the types of data analysis. The literature describes three forms characterized by their objectives, and two further orientations that describe what the analysis is for.

          The three forms of data analysis

          The forms of data analysis are characterized by their respective objectives:

          FormObjectiveThe question it answersA university-level illustration
          Descriptive statisticsTo organize and summarize data.What does the data I have actually say?"Across eight weeks, the canteen sold an average of 62 servings of rice meals per day."
          Exploratory data analysisTo find hidden patterns in the data in order to formulate new hypotheses.What is going on in here that I did not expect?"Friday sales look different from the other days — there may be a weekly pattern worth testing."
          Inferential statisticsTo describe observations by fitting them to statistical models and test the plausibility of hypotheses based on data.Does this pattern hold beyond the data I happen to have, and how sure can I be?"Based on this sample, is the Friday difference real, or could it plausibly be chance?"

          When exploratory data analyses are performed on a large scale, they are also referred to as data mining, described as the process of discovering interesting patterns from massive amounts of data, or the practice of searching through large amounts of computerized data to find useful patterns or trends.

          Descriptive statistics in more detail

          Descriptive statistics provide a summary of the key characteristics of a dataset. They include measures of central tendency such as the mean and median, which give insight into the typical values, and measures of dispersion such as variance and standard deviation, which describe the spread and variability of the data points. Distributional properties can be explored through histograms, which display the frequency distribution, and box plots, which show spread and skewness and highlight outliers and the interquartile range. These statistics form the foundational step in understanding the basic features and quality of a dataset before deeper analysis.

          Exploratory data analysis in more detail

          EDA enables analysts to gain insight into the underlying patterns, trends, and relationships within a dataset. Its main tools are:

          • Descriptive statistics — central tendency, dispersion, histograms, box plots.
          • Data visualization — scatter plots to identify relationships between two numerical variables, histograms for the distribution of a single variable, bar charts to compare categorical data, and heatmaps to show the magnitude of values across two dimensions.
          • Correlation analysis — quantifying the strength and direction of relationships between variables.
          • Clustering and dimensionality reduction — identifying natural groupings within the data and simplifying data with many variables.

          The predictive maintenance case shows why EDA is not optional: it was through EDA that the company discovered that certain patterns in sensor readings preceded equipment failures, which laid the groundwork for the predictive models that followed. The discovery came before the model, not from it.

          Inference and prediction

          Statistical inference involves drawing conclusions or making predictions about a population based on sample data. Point estimation provides single-value estimates of population parameters such as the mean or a proportion; interval estimation, such as a confidence interval, offers a range of values within which the parameter is expected to lie, providing a measure of uncertainty. Techniques like these allow findings to be generalized from samples to broader populations while accounting for variability and uncertainty.

          Alongside inference sits prediction. Predictive analytics involves using historical data and statistical techniques to forecast future events or outcomes, enabling organizations to anticipate trends and identify patterns. A great deal of modern prediction is done with machine learning, a subset of artificial intelligence focused on algorithms and models that can learn from data and make predictions or decisions without being explicitly programmed — systems that operate by rules that were not given explicitly but were mostly learned from training data.

          Four questions data can answer

          A useful ladder describes how the field is developing: analytics is moving beyond descriptive and diagnostic analyses toward predictive and prescriptive insights, enabling organizations not only to understand what has happened but also to anticipate what is likely to happen and to recommend optimal courses of action. Predictive analytics forecasts trends and projects outcomes based on existing patterns; prescriptive analytics goes a step further, recommending specific actions based on those predictions.

          OrientationThe question it answersNote
          DescriptiveWhat has happened?A fully developed form of analysis, treated in detail in this chapter.
          DiagnosticWhy did it happen?Often listed alongside the others in industry frameworks; introduced here by name and developed later in the course.

          #Lesson 1.9 Visualizing and Communicating Insights

          Results from data analysis can be communicated to humans through language and through visual and graphical representations, and the subfield of data analysis that focuses on graphical representation is called data visualization. More formally, data visualization is the graphical representation of data to facilitate understanding, exploration, and communication of insights, using charts, graphs, heatmaps, and interactive dashboards.

          Why a chart can beat a table

          A classic example: a line diagram shows air temperature measured two metres above the ground in one district of Berlin for the year 2018, with a grey ribbon representing the daily variation between minimum and maximum temperature. Two findings can easily be seen from it: the coldest days that year were in late February and early March; and although it is obviously colder in winter than in summer, the chart also shows how much more daytime and nighttime temperatures differ in summer than in winter — the grey ribbon is much wider in the summer. The decisive observation: the same information could not as easily or efficiently be determined from a table of measurements.

          That is the argument for visualization in one sentence. Not that charts are prettier, but that some true things are practically invisible in a table and obvious in a picture. Worked Example 4 asks you to reason about that example yourself.

          Five principles for honest visuals

          Data visualization is more than creating aesthetically pleasing charts; it is about effectively communicating complex information clearly and concisely. The principles are these:

          PrincipleWhat it requires
          Clarity and simplicityVisualizations should be clear, concise, and easy to understand. Avoid clutter and unnecessary complexity. Remove elements that do not support comprehension. Use direct labels and clean axes.
          Accuracy and integrityEnsure visualizations accurately represent the underlying data without distorting or misleading. Use appropriate scales, labels, and annotations. Misrepresentation leads not only to misunderstanding but to decisions based on faulty interpretations.
          Relevance and contextVisualizations should be relevant to the audience and the intended message. Provide context and background so viewers can interpret the data; comparisons and benchmarks make insights more actionable.
          Use of colour and visual elementsChoose colours, fonts, and visual elements to enhance readability. Use palettes accessible to all viewers and avoid colour used purely for decoration. Colour should highlight significant data points and guide attention.
          Interactivity and engagementInteractive elements such as tooltips, filters, and drill-downs let viewers explore data dynamically, and should be intuitive rather than complicating.

          Of the five, accuracy and integrity is the one with an ethical edge. A cluttered chart wastes a reader’s time; a distorted axis changes what they believe. The stakes are plain: misrepresentation can lead to decisions that are based on faulty interpretations.

          Beyond the visuals themselves, effective communication requires tailoring the message to the audience, telling compelling stories with data, using multiple channels, soliciting feedback, and fostering data literacy.

          #Lesson 1.10 Ethical and Responsible Use of Data

          Data privacy and ethical principles guide the responsible collection, handling, and analysis of data, ensuring that individuals’ rights are protected and that analytics practices adhere to legal and moral standards. This lesson introduces those principles; the course develops them further in a later chapter devoted to data privacy and ethics.

          The framing to hold on to is this: data collection and usage raise important ethical questions regarding privacy, consent, and the potential for misuse of personal information. Behind almost every row in a dataset about people is a person who did not necessarily agree to be a row.

          Four duties in collecting and using data

          DutyWhat it requires
          Privacy and consentRespect individuals’ right to privacy and obtain informed consent before collecting, storing, or processing their personal data. Provide clear and transparent information about the purpose of collection, how the data will be used, and any potential risks or implications. Privacy policies should be understandable and accessible, and consent should be voluntary and explicit, including detail about any sharing with third parties.
          Data ownership and controlClarify ownership and control rights over collected data, especially where data comes from multiple sources or is shared. Ensure individuals have the right to access, modify, or delete their personal data, and provide mechanisms that let them exercise those rights.
          Data securitySafeguard collected data against unauthorized access, disclosure, or misuse through measures such as encryption, access controls, and regular security audits, minimizing the risk of breaches and protecting sensitive information.
          Responsible data usageUse data in ways that avoid harm, discrimination, or unfair treatment of individuals or groups. Consider the broader societal implications of data-driven decisions and aim to maximize benefit while minimizing harm.

          Fairness, bias, and transparency

          Algorithms and machine learning models can inadvertently perpetuate biases and unfairness if they are not designed and implemented thoughtfully. Four responses are described:

          • Fairness in algorithm design. Ensure algorithms treat all individuals fairly and without bias regardless of race, gender, ethnicity, or other protected characteristics, and integrate diverse perspectives during design.
          • Bias detection and mitigation. Identify and reduce bias in data, algorithms, and decision processes through auditing and debiasing, and review collection practices and deployment strategies so they do not amplify bias.
          • Transparency and explainability. Provide clear explanations of how algorithms work, how decisions are made, and the potential implications for the people affected, in a form that non-experts can also assess.
          • Algorithmic impact assessments. Evaluate the social, ethical, and legal implications of a system before and during deployment, engaging those who may be affected.

          Legal and regulatory frameworks sit alongside these duties. Three families matter: data protection laws, which carry principles of data minimization, purpose limitation, and data subject rights; anti-discrimination laws, which forbid practices producing disparate impacts based on protected characteristics; and intellectual property rights, which require appropriate permissions and licences for third-party data sources and documentation of where data came from.

          A note on the law

          This chapter teaches the principles — privacy, consent, ownership, security, fairness, transparency — as ethics. The specific legal duties that implement them differ by jurisdiction; in the Philippines they are set out in Republic Act No. 10173, the Data Privacy Act of 2012, which a later chapter of this course treats in full. When you handle personal data in any project, check the current legal requirements that apply to you, not only the principles.

          A test you can apply now

          Before collecting anything about another person, ask five questions drawn directly from the duties above: Do they know? Did they agree, knowing the purpose? Can they get it back, correct it, or have it deleted? Is it protected while I hold it? Could this use harm or unfairly disadvantage them or a group they belong to? If any answer is unsatisfactory, the problem is with the plan, not with the question.

          Problem or task. A health survey has missing height and weight values. Complete the dataset by mean substitution, then repeat using imputation classes, and compare what each choice does to the summary you would report.

          Given information. A small sample of results from a 2018 telephone survey, in which "NA" marks a missing value because the respondent provided no data, invalid data, or insufficient data:

          sexage (years)height (cm)weight (kg)
          male3517098
          male66NANA
          female67155NA

          Source: missing attribute values.

          The survey supplies the figures needed for substitution. The mean body height and mean body weight determined from the entire dataset as provided by the survey are 170 cm and 82 kg respectively. Note carefully that these means come from the full dataset, not from the three rows shown; that is why they can be used to fill the gaps in those rows.

          Step-by-step solution

          Step 1. Confirm that imputation is the right response. The values are missing, not invalid, so validation is not the operation needed. Missing attribute values can lead to bias in the results of data analysis, and imputation completes the data by replacing them with synthetic but plausible values. The alternative is complete case analysis, which excludes records missing relevant values.

          Step 2. Apply mean substitution using the overall means. Replace every missing height with 170 cm and every missing weight with 82 kg. The result is the mean-substituted dataset:

          sexage (years)height (cm)weight (kg)
          male3517098
          male6617082
          female6715582

          Mean substitution. The imputed values are shown in the second and third rows.

          Step 3. Refine using imputation classes. The dataset can be partitioned into convenient imputation classes so that mean substitution is applied within each class. Taking into account that male respondents typically have larger body weight and body height, the class means of the survey give 178 cm and 90 kg as the substitutions for the male respondent, and 75 kg for the female respondent. Her height of 155 cm is not imputed at all, because it was observed in the first place; only her weight was missing. Substituting within class gives:

          sexage (years)height (cm)weight (kg)
          male3517098
          male6617890
          female6715575

          Use of imputation classes.

          Step 4. Compute the mean weight of these three respondents under each treatment. This is the number a report would actually quote, so it is the number to compare.

          TreatmentWeights usedComputationMean weight (kg)
          Complete case analysis98 only (two records excluded)98 ÷ 198.00
          Mean substitution, overall98, 82, 82(98 + 82 + 82) ÷ 3 = 262 ÷ 387.33
          Mean substitution, by class98, 90, 75(98 + 90 + 75) ÷ 3 = 263 ÷ 387.67

          Computed from the tables above; only the arithmetic is performed here.

          Interpretation of the result

          Three defensible procedures applied to the same three respondents produce three different reported averages, from 87.33 kg to 98.00 kg. The gap between the two imputation methods is small here, but the gap between imputing and discarding is large — and it is large in a predictable direction, because the discarded records were exactly the ones with missing information.

          This is why the primary goal of data imputation is not to determine the correct attribute values but to supplement the data in order to improve the results. The imputed 90 kg is not this man’s weight. It is a placeholder chosen so that the summary is less distorted than it would be if he vanished from the dataset entirely. An honest report says which method was used.

          Common mistake to avoid

          Reporting an imputed figure as if it were observed. Imputed values are synthetic but plausible values, and a reader who is not told this will over-trust the result. State the method, the number of values imputed, and the class used, every time.

          A second, quieter mistake: ignoring imputation classes when the groups genuinely differ. Substituting the overall mean weight of 82 kg for a male respondent pulls his record toward the female respondents in the dataset, which is the opposite of what the class information supports.

          Reflection question

          Suppose 40 percent of the respondents in a survey left weight blank, and that people who are uncomfortable about their weight are the most likely to skip the question. Does mean substitution fix the problem or hide it? Answer using the word "bias".

          #Worked Example 3 Validating, Standardizing, and Deduplicating a Record

          Problem or task. Two data sources describe what appears to be the same person, in different formats. Produce one clean, standardized record, and state what remains uncertain.

          Given information. Table C.3 in Lesson 1.7: name recorded as "Rose Smith" and "Smith, Rose"; phone as "+498932168" and "+4989-321-68"; date of birth as "1981-12-07" and "12/07/1981"; organization as "Example Incorporated" and "Example Inc."; body height as "180 cm" and "5’ 11”".

          Step-by-step solution

          Step 1. Validate before standardizing. Check each value against syntax, range, and plausibility rules. Adopt the syntax rule that a date of birth must be stored as YYYY-MM-DD with a four-digit year, two-digit month, and two-digit day, and the range rule 1 ≤ MM ≤ 12. Under this rule "1981-12-07" is valid. "12/07/1981" is not valid in the target syntax, but it is not implausible — it is merely in a different format, so it is a candidate for standardization rather than deletion.

          Step 2. Choose one target syntax and one unit per attribute. Standardization converts attribute values into a specified target syntax and common units of measurement. Choose: date as YYYY-MM-DD; length in centimetres; name as a single agreed order; phone digits without separators; organization name in one agreed spelling.

          Step 3. Convert. Map "12/07/1981" onto the target syntax. Convert "5’ 11”" into centimetres so that both height records are expressed in the same unit, converting all body heights across data sources to a single unit. Remove the separators from the second phone number so both read as one digit string.

          Step 4. Decide whether the two records are duplicates. Deduplication is the task of identifying the records that describe a single entity in order to merge them into a single representative record. Here the two records agree on a standardized name, a standardized phone number, and, once standardized, a birth date and an organization. That agreement is what justifies merging them into one representative record.

          Step 5. Record what you did. Keep the original raw values, since maintaining the original raw data or a backup of it is the historical record called data provenance, which helps track down the cause of data quality issues.

          Interpretation of the result

          One clean record now replaces two partial ones. Notice the order that made this possible: the two records could not be recognised as duplicates until after they were standardized, because before standardization they disagree in every single field. Standardization is not cosmetic tidying; it is what makes matching possible at all.

          One genuine uncertainty remains, and it should be reported rather than hidden. The date "12/07/1981" is unambiguous only if you already know whether the writer recorded month first or day first. The record pairs it with 1981-12-07, which supports reading it as 7 December 1981 — but that inference depends on the pairing, not on the string itself. The standard caution applies: repairing a value into valid form is only advisable if the data’s provenance and the data generating process imply that the result is not only valid but also accurate.

          Common mistake to avoid

          Deduplicating before standardizing. Records that describe one entity but are written differently will not match, so they survive as duplicates and inflate every count computed afterwards. The patent-database example makes the cost concrete: one company appears under a dozen spellings, so even counting its patents becomes non-trivial.

          A second mistake: silently choosing an interpretation for an ambiguous date instead of flagging it. Ambiguity that you resolve by guessing does not disappear; it just becomes invisible.

          Reflection question

          You standardize a set of records and the number of distinct people in your dataset drops from 240 to 198. Is that a data loss or a data improvement? Explain what you would check before reporting either number.

          #Worked Example 4 Reading a Chart Correctly

          Problem or task. Interpret a described line diagram, and explain why the chart was a better choice than a table for the findings it carries.

          Given information. A line diagram shows air temperature measured at two metres above the ground in the district of Tegel in Berlin, Germany, for the year 2018. The horizontal axis runs from January to December. The vertical axis is air temperature in degrees Celsius, running from below −10 to above 30. A grey ribbon represents the daily variation between the minimum and maximum temperatures.

          Step-by-step solution

          Step 1. Read the axes before reading the line. Identify what is measured (air temperature in °C), where (two metres above ground, one district), when (the year 2018), and at what granularity (daily minimum and maximum). Every later statement is limited by these four facts.

          Step 2. Read the level of the line. The chart reports the first finding directly: the coldest days that year were in late February and early March.

          Step 3. Read the width of the ribbon, not only the height of the line. The ribbon encodes a second variable — the daily spread between minimum and maximum. Reading the ribbon shows that although it is obviously colder in winter than in summer, the chart also illustrates how much more the daytime and nighttime temperatures differ in summer compared with the winter: the grey ribbon is much wider in the summer than in the winter.

          Step 4. Ask what the chart does not say. It describes one district in one year. It supports no claim about other locations, other years, or causes. Nothing in the data extends it further.

          Interpretation of the result

          Two distinct findings come out of one picture, and they are of different kinds. The first — where the coldest days fell — could be recovered from a table by scanning for the minimum. The second could not, in practice, because it is a statement about how a gap changes across a year, and a reader would have to compute hundreds of differences mentally to see it. That is precisely the point of visualization: the same information could not as easily or efficiently be determined from a table of measurements.

          This also illustrates the visualization principle of clarity and simplicity, which asks that a chart convey key insights succinctly. The ribbon adds a second variable without adding a second chart.

          Common mistake to avoid

          Reporting the pattern as a cause. The chart shows that summer days have a wider daily temperature range; it does not explain why. Accuracy and integrity require that a visualization represent the underlying data without distorting or misleading, and a caption that supplies an unsupported explanation misleads just as effectively as a distorted axis.

          A second mistake: quietly dropping the location and the year when repeating the finding. "Days vary more in summer" is a much larger claim than the chart supports.

          Reflection question

          Rewrite the second finding as a single sentence a non-specialist reader would understand, keeping every limitation intact. Then explain which word in your sentence is doing the most work to keep it honest.

          #Skill 1 Asking a Meaningful Question of Data

          Skill description. Turning a vague concern into a question that data could actually answer — one that names what is measured, about whom, and over what period.

          Why this skill matters. The lifecycle begins with acquisition, and effective acquisition strategies are critical for ensuring a dataset that accurately represents the phenomena under study.           Higher-order thinking prompt

          A question can be perfectly answerable and still not worth answering. Write one answerable question about your university that you would refuse to investigate, and justify the refusal using a duty from Lesson 1.10.

          #Skill 2 Identifying Appropriate Data and Its Source

          Skill description. Choosing which data would actually answer your question, and deciding where it will come from and how it will be acquired.

          Why this skill matters. Understanding the availability, reliability, and legality of data sources is crucial for ensuring the quality and relevance of the collected data. Data that is available but irrelevant, or relevant but not legitimately obtainable, is not usable data.

          Guided practice. For one of your questions from Skill 1, work through the six source types — internal databases, external APIs, web scraping, sensor networks, social media platforms, third-party providers — and rule each one in or out with a reason.

          Two datasets could answer your question: one is easy to obtain but only partly relevant, the other is highly relevant but harder to obtain legitimately. Argue for one, and state the condition under which you would change your mind.

          #Skill 3 Organizing Data into a Usable Structure

          Skill description. Turning collected records into a dataset with defined attributes, one row per statistical unit, and a primary key.

          Why this skill matters. A dataset is a collection of data items structured in the same way; rows are data records and columns are attributes, with the primary key uniquely identifying each entity. Most statistical work requires the data in rectangular form, and unstructured raw data must be processed into structured form before that work can begin.

          Guided practice. Using Table C.1 in Lesson 1.4, state the statistical unit, list the attributes with their data types, and explain why no single existing column is a primary key for a row.

          Independent practice. Design the structure of a dataset for your question from Skill 1. Produce a table of attribute names, data types, valid ranges, and units. Add a primary key column and explain what it guarantees.

          Higher-order thinking prompt

          Someone proposes storing your data with one row per person and one column per week, rather than one row per person-week. Compare the two layouts. Which questions become easy under each, and which become awkward?

          #Skill 4 Checking Data Quality

          Skill description. Profiling a dataset against the six data quality dimensions and reporting the defects as counts or proportions.

          Why this skill matters. A high-quality dataset is a prerequisite for generating trusted and useful knowledge, and data profiling is the practice of collecting metrics in order to assess data quality. A defect you have not measured is a defect you cannot argue about.

          Guided practice. Take a small list of records supplied by your instructor. Together, write one check for each of the six dimensions — completeness, consistency, validity, uniqueness, accuracy, timeliness — phrased as a probing question.

          Independent practice. Run all six checks and report each result as a count and a proportion, as metrics, for example the proportion of records with an invalid postal code matched against a fixed pattern.

          Higher-order thinking prompt

          Rank your six results by how much each defect threatens the specific use you have in mind, and justify the ranking using the principle that the significance of each dimension depends on the use case at hand.

          #Skill 5 Cleaning Data and Justifying Every Change

          Skill description. Applying validation, standardization, imputation, augmentation, and deduplication, keeping the raw data intact and logging each decision.

          Why this skill matters. Processing data accurately and securely means creating backups and cleaning datasets to address missing or inconsistent entries, and keeping a historical record of preprocessing — including the original raw data or a backup — which is called data provenance.

          Guided practice. Redo Worked Example 3 with a different record supplied by your instructor. Do the five operations in the order given at the end of Lesson 1.7, and write one line of justification for each change.

          Independent practice. Clean a messy dataset supplied by your instructor. Submit three files or sections: the untouched raw data, the cleaned data, and a cleaning log stating what was changed, why, and under which operation.

          Higher-order thinking prompt

          One of your cleaning decisions changed a headline number by more than ten percent. Defend the decision to a sceptical reader who suspects you cleaned the data until it said what you wanted.

          Skill description. Computing and reporting measures of central tendency and dispersion, and knowing which are meaningful for which data type.

          Why this skill matters. Descriptive statistics summarize the key characteristics of a dataset through measures of central tendency such as mean and median and measures of dispersion such as variance and standard deviation, and they form the foundational step in understanding the basic features and quality of a dataset before deeper analysis.

          Skill description. Saying what a pattern shows, and saying just as clearly what it does not show.

          Why this skill matters. Exploratory data analysis finds hidden patterns in order to formulate new hypotheses; it produces candidates for investigation, not conclusions. Inferential statistics is what tests the plausibility of a hypothesis based on data.

          Skill description. Stating the boundaries of a dataset: whom it covers, when, how completely, and what it therefore cannot answer.

          #G. Chapter Summary

          #Main concepts learned

          • What the field is. Data are a formalized representation of information, or factual information used as a basis for reasoning, discussion, or calculation; raw facts, figures, and statistics collected for analysis. Data analysis obtains relevant information and useful knowledge by systematic organization, statistical processing, and graphical representation. The main function of data science is the collection, processing, interpretation, and communication of data with the goal of obtaining robust and useful knowledge.
          • Why it matters. Data-driven decision-making lets organizations identify market trends and consumer preferences, enhance operational efficiency, mitigate risks and uncertainties, and drive innovation and competitive advantage; the practice appears in retail, healthcare, finance, manufacturing, and entertainment.
          • Data types. Data is structured or unstructured. Structured data is numeric — continuous or discrete — or categorical, with nominal, ordinal, and binary as its forms. The data type determines the appropriate display, analysis, and model.
          • The data life cycle. Data acquisition, data preparation, exploratory data analysis, model development, model evaluation, and deployment and monitoring — an iterative process, in which monitoring is essential because performance can degrade over time.
          • Quality and cleaning. Quality has six dimensions: completeness, consistency, validity, uniqueness, accuracy, timeliness — and its significance depends on the intended use. Cleaning comprises validation, standardization, imputation, augmentation, and deduplication, supported by transformation and integration, and protected by data provenance.
          • Types of analysis. Descriptive statistics organize and summarize; exploratory data analysis finds hidden patterns to form hypotheses; inferential statistics fits models and tests the plausibility of hypotheses. Predictive analytics forecasts outcomes and prescriptive analytics recommends actions.

          #Key skills developed

          Asking an answerable question; identifying appropriate data and its source; organizing data into records and attributes with a primary key; profiling a dataset against six quality dimensions; cleaning data and justifying every change; summarizing data appropriately for its type; choosing and reading a visualization; interpreting patterns without overclaiming; stating limitations honestly; and communicating a finding responsibly.

          #Ethical reminders

          • Obtain informed consent, and tell people the purpose of collection, how the data will be used, and any risks.
          • Respect ownership and control: people may access, modify, or delete their personal data.
          • Secure what you hold, through measures such as access controls and regular review.
          • Use data in ways that avoid harm, discrimination, or unfair treatment.
          • Keep the raw data and a record of every change, so your work can be checked.
          • Do not distort. A visualization must represent the underlying data without misleading, because misrepresentation produces decisions based on faulty interpretations.

          #Synthesis: data analytics and critical thinking

          Every stage of this chapter is a place where a person can be careless and still produce a confident-looking result. You can collect data that does not represent what you claim to study; you can treat an identifier as a quantity and compute a meaningless average; you can leave duplicates in and miscount; you can impute a missing value and then forget you invented it; you can find a pattern in exploration and announce it as proven; you can draw a chart with a distorted scale and change what a reader believes. What protects against all of these is not a formula but a habit of mind — the habit of asking what the data actually is, what was done to it, what the result can and cannot support, and whom it affects. That habit is critical thinking, and data analytics is one of the few subjects in which its absence produces a visible, checkable error. The definition given at the start of this chapter ends with the words "robust and useful knowledge"; robustness is the part that critical thinking supplies, and without it the rest of the work is only arithmetic performed confidently in the wrong direction.

          #K. Performance Task

          #“From Raw Records to a Defensible Recommendation”

          Important note on the dataset — read before assigning

          This performance task is written as a template: the instructor supplies the dataset, so the task can be run against local, current, and relevant records rather than a canned file.

          The dataset supplied should be a real, messy, tabular file of roughly 80 to 400 records with at least six attributes covering more than one data type, and should contain genuine defects — missing values, inconsistent formats, at least one duplicate, and at least one implausible value — because those are what the task assesses. Where the dataset contains information about identifiable people, the instructor is responsible for ensuring it may lawfully and ethically be used for coursework, consistent with the duties in Lesson 1.10.

          The situation

          An organization in your community keeps records but does not use them. Its manager has a decision to make and has been making it by instinct. Your team has been given a copy of the organization’s records and asked to turn them into a recommendation the manager can act on and defend.

          The pattern you are being asked to reproduce is documented in real cases. A retail chain in exactly this position collected data from its sales transactions, inventory levels, and market trends; preprocessed, cleaned, and transformed it; used analytics to identify demand patterns; and implemented visualization to communicate insights across departments — reducing excess inventory and minimizing stockouts as a result. Your task is the same shape at project scale.

          Student output

          Each team submits four things:

          1. A data package containing the untouched raw file, the cleaned file, and a cleaning log recording every change, its reason, and the operation it belongs to.
          2. A data documentation sheet covering the context of collection, a description of every attribute with its data type, valid range, and unit, an evaluation of data quality, and the terms under which the data may be used.
          3. A two-page findings brief containing the question, the summary statistics, at least two charts, the interpretation, the limitations, and one clearly stated recommendation.
          4. A five-minute oral presentation of the brief, followed by questions.

          Required data analytics process

          The team must move through the data life cycle and must be able to show where each stage happened.
